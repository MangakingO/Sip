On the launch sequence

  • The cup tips, the brick wave rises from the bottom with a curved leading edge, and the wordmark and line ride up with it. The wave holds for roughly a second and a quarter before the app appears — long enough to read the line, short enough not to resent.
  • Total length is about 3.9 seconds. That's long for a splash. If it starts to grate, cut the hold rather than the wave; the wave is the part that reads as craft.

The screen you were missing

There was no screen for the thing the whole app is about. Feed, map, and profile are all browsing — none of them answer "who makes the best oat milk latte in Dallas." That's now its own tab, with city and rating sorts and a flag on the top result. If a grader or an investor opens this app once, that's the screen that has to exist.

Comments, and the rule behind them

  • Replies hang off a review and nothing else. There is no blank composer anywhere in the app — the Post tab opens straight into step 1 of a review. If you want to say something on Sip, you have to have drunk something first.
  • That rule is the moderation strategy, not just a product choice. Most of what goes wrong on social apps arrives through free-form posting: spam, self-promotion by shops, picked fights. Requiring a photo, a location, and a rating before anyone can speak raises the cost of a junk post from seconds to a real trip.
  • The questions people actually ask are logistical. Which matcha, how sweet, what time do they open. Those are answerable by the person who was just there, which is why threading replies under the review beats a separate Q&A.
  • The author gets a marker. When a reviewer answers a question about their own drink, that reply carries more weight than a bystander's guess, and it should be visible without reading the handle.
  • One thing to decide: whether replies count toward rank. I'd say no — ranking on replies turns a review app into a comment farm. Ranks stay tied to reviews with a photo and a note.

Three other things worth adding

  • A saved list. Yelp's bookmark, basically. Someone reads a review at 11pm and wants it on Saturday. Cheap to build, and it's the feature that brings people back without a notification.
  • Review again. Your schema already handles it — posting the same unmodified drink updates the existing review. But there's no UI for it, so a user who revisits a shop will think the app is broken when their second review vanishes. Worth a visible "update your review" state.
  • An empty state with a job. A new user in a city with no reviews sees nothing, decides the app is dead, and leaves. That screen should name the nearest shop with no reviews yet and ask them to be first. It converts the weakest moment in the product into the one that generates your content.

Movement, and the maths behind it

  • Green up, red down, a dash for no change, amber NEW for a first appearance. The number is places moved, not score change — "▲ 2" means it climbed two positions.
  • Ranking on raw average breaks immediately: a drink with one five-star review outranks one with two hundred at 4.8. Use a weighted score — a Bayesian average that pulls low-count drinks toward the city mean until they earn their position. Otherwise your leaderboard is noise and every new drink debuts at number one.
  • Snapshot the standings weekly into a table and compare. Computing movement live against a moving window produces positions that flicker by the hour, which makes the board feel untrustworthy.
  • Trending is deliberately a different metric: rate of new reviews, not rating. A 4.2 that forty people tried this week is more interesting than a 4.9 nobody has ordered since August. Keep the two boards separate or they collapse into each other.

On the Google Places integration

  • Hours and photos come from Places; the menu does not. There is no structured menu field. You get regularOpeningHours, photos, websiteUri, and nationalPhoneNumber. So the "menu" on this screen is your own drink list built from reviews — which is better anyway, since it carries ratings their real menu never will.
  • Caching rules shape the architecture. Google lets you store place_id indefinitely but not the other fields, so hours and photos get fetched near-live and cached only briefly. Practically: store place_id in RDS, fetch details through a Lambda, cache the response in ElastiCache or a short-TTL DynamoDB table.
  • Photos are billed per request and each one needs the attribution Google returns. The small "Photo via Google" line on the hero is a requirement, not decoration.
  • Open/closed status is computed from the hours in the user's timezone, not handed to you as a boolean in the basic response. Easy to get wrong across midnight closes — a shop open until 1 AM reads as closed all evening if you compare naively.

The three additions

  • Saved shows open-now status per entry, which is what turns a saved list into something you act on at 2pm on a Saturday rather than a list you never reopen.
  • Review again explains the constraint instead of just enforcing it, and offers the modified-order escape hatch — which is the honest answer when someone genuinely did order it differently this time.
  • The empty state names real shops and asks for a first review. It also offers a way back to a populated city, so a new user in a dead area isn't trapped looking at nothing.
